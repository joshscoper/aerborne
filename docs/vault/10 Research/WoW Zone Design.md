# WoW zone design

What World of Warcraft's zones do well, and the rule we take for the Reach. Sourced points cite [[Sources]];
"(play)" marks points from playing and studying the game rather than a developer quote.

## What the developers said
- **The world is the strongest asset.** Tom Chilton (GDC 2011) on remaking the old zones for Cataclysm: the
  "world" part of WoW had to survive the overhaul, but the original had very poor flow. Quests "ping-ponged"
  players across the map because there was no top-down plan. *(S3)*
- **Plan top-down, with a map and a flow chart.** Cataclysm zones started as an illustrator's map of the redone
  zone plus a designer flow chart of where the player goes. *(S4)*
- **Zones are characters with a history.** Julian Morris (Artcraft, 2014): the team plans "the rise and fall of
  ancient cultures" in a zone so the land shows its past; ruins are the obvious tool (McInerney). Settlements
  are the staging that props are later placed in, so macro placement drives all the detail. *(S5)*
- **Crossing a border changes how progress feels.** Moving between zones and subzones changes the player's sense
  of progression (McInerney). *(S5)*
- **Linear zones are unpopular.** John Staats: Darkshore and Felwood, long strips, were disliked because trips back
  to turn in were long. *(S6)*
- **Keep landmarks.** Nagrand's redesign kept the key landmarks of the old zone. *(S7)*
- **Hide things up high.** Draenor's level designers put treasure on mountaintops where players work out their own
  route up, with a chest as the prize at the landing spot. *(S7)*

## How a WoW zone is put together (play)
- **One theme, one palette, one silhouette.** Elwynn is gold-green forest, Westfall dry yellow fields and
  scarecrows, Duskwood black trees and fog. You know where you are from one screenshot.
- **A bowl.** Zones sit in a ring of mountains with two or three **passes** (often a road through a gap, a bridge
  or a tunnel) that act as doors to the next zone. The pass is itself a landmark.
- **A road is the spine.** The main road runs from the entry pass through every hub to the exit pass. Side
  content hangs off it within sight. Signposts at every fork name the next town.
- **Hubs every few minutes.** A starter zone has a main town plus one or two outposts. Each is lit, has an inn,
  a vendor, a flight point and enough quests (Chilton called Westfall's old hub "inadequate": a few quests, an
  inn and a vendor). *(S3)*
- **A landmark you can see from far away** (a tower, a giant tree, a burning crater). You see the next goal
  before you get the quest for it.
- **The zone tells one story with a mystery** and the climax is a dungeon: Westfall's Defias → the Deadmines.
  The dungeon's door is physically in the zone, often under the story's biggest landmark.
- **Subzones** are named areas of 50 to 150 m with their own identity (a mill, a farmstead, a mine, a camp).
  Entering one flashes its name. Each holds one mob family and one or two quest objectives.
- **The world reacts.** Phasing changes the zone as the story advances (Afrasiabi: it began as a bug fix and
  became a storytelling tool). *(S3)* We can do a light version: NPCs move, a camp burns, a gate opens.

## Rules for the Reach
1. Each zone is a **bowl** with its own palette, silhouette and history; borders are ridges, cliffs, rivers or a
   sky gap, crossed at 2–3 named passes.
2. **A road spine** from entry pass through hubs to exit pass, with signposts. Nothing important is out of sight
   of the road for long.
3. **A main hub and one or two outposts per zone**, each with an innkeeper, a vendor, a skiff (flight) point,
   quests, and light.
4. **Zone story with a mystery**, climaxing at the expedition door in that zone ([[Main Story]]).
5. **A far landmark per zone**, and the Ashen Scar's smoke column visible from everywhere as the final goal.
6. **Subzones** 50–150 blocks, each named, each with one mob family and one job to do.
7. **Branch, don't line.** Zones are roughly round with hubs near the middle, so turn-in trips stay short.
8. **Hide rewards high and low** (mountain tops, behind falls, under the continent's lip): treasure chests.
